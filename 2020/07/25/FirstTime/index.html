Security Fundamentals: Threats, Risk, and Defensive Controls

July 25, 2020•8 min read

Security is not only a collection of tools. It is a way of thinking about assets, threats, weaknesses, impact, and the controls that reduce harm.

1. Start with the foundations

Before choosing a tool, identify what needs protection and why it matters.

  • Assets: data, systems, applications, users, and business processes.
  • Threats: events or actors that could cause harm.
  • Vulnerabilities: weaknesses that can be exploited.
  • Risk: the likelihood and impact of a threat exploiting a weakness.

2. Use layered controls

Controls should work together rather than depend on one perfect defense.

  • Preventive: reduce the chance of an incident.
  • Detective: reveal suspicious activity.
  • Corrective: restore service and reduce impact.
  • Compensating: provide alternate protection when a primary control is unavailable.
Good security does not eliminate every risk. It makes important risks visible, manageable, and recoverable.

3. Protect identity and access

Authentication proves who someone is. Authorization decides what that identity may do. Together they support least privilege and role-based access.

  • Use strong, unique credentials.
  • Require multifactor authentication for sensitive access.
  • Review permissions regularly.
  • Remove access when roles change.

4. Assess business risk

Risk decisions should consider likelihood, impact, dependencies, recovery capability, and the value of the affected asset.

TermQuestion it answers
RTOHow quickly must the service be restored?
RPOHow much recent data can the business afford to lose?
MTDHow long can the business tolerate disruption?

5. Understand common threats

Threat actors differ in motivation and capability. Common categories include opportunistic attackers, organized crime, insiders, hacktivists, and nation-state groups.

Social engineering deserves special attention because attackers often exploit urgency, authority, familiarity, fear, or scarcity rather than a technical vulnerability.

6. Test and improve

Security assessment should be continuous. Code review, configuration review, vulnerability scanning, logging, and authorized penetration testing help identify weaknesses before they become incidents.

  • Document the scope and authorization.
  • Prioritize findings by business impact.
  • Track remediation to completion.
  • Retest important fixes.

7. Key takeaways

  1. Know what you are protecting.
  2. Expect weaknesses and design layers.
  3. Keep access limited and observable.
  4. Plan for recovery, not only prevention.
  5. Turn every assessment into an improvement.

Security becomes stronger when it is treated as an ongoing engineering practice rather than a one-time project.
